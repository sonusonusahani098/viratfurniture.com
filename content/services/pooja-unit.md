In most Surat homes, the mandir is the most loved and least planned corner — a deity shelf that started temporary a decade ago, samagri in a plastic box nearby, the diya balanced with practised care. Families rarely upgrade it because it feels wrong to disturb. But a proper pooja unit doesn't disturb the sanctity of the corner; it honours it — gives the deities their own crafted space, the rituals their storage, and the daily aarti a setting that matches its importance in the home.

Pooja units are among the most personal pieces our workshop makes, and we build them with that weight in mind.

## From a shelf to a shrine — sized to your home

**Wall-mounted mandirs** are the workhorse for apartment living: a crafted unit at eye level, a ledge for the diya and thali, a drawer beneath for the samagri, doors that respectfully close over the space when needed. No floor area claimed at all.

**Floor-standing units** suit homes with a dedicated corner — broader deity platforms, more storage, and presence enough to anchor the room. **Niche conversions** turn an existing alcove into a finished mandir with panelling, shelf and lighting. And for homes with a full pooja room, we do **complete fit-outs** — platform, screens, storage and seating designed together, often with a jali [partition](/services/wooden-partition) that lets the sacred space breathe within the home's open plan.

## Traditional hands, or modern calm — your call

Two design languages cover most requests. The **traditional**: arch tops, jali lattice doors, bell accents, turned details and rich polished wood tones — a mandir that would look at home in a haveli. The **contemporary**: clean rectangular forms, warm backlit panels, slat backgrounds and minimal hardware — sacred but serene, matched to a modern flat's interiors.

Both come off the same benches at our Palanpur workshop. Jali work, arch profiles and detailing are where two decades of carpentry actually show — machine-cut lattice has its place, but the fitting and finishing still take a craftsman's hand.

## Designed around the rituals themselves

A pooja unit succeeds in the daily details: the diya ledge positioned so the flame is safe and the aarti thali has somewhere to rest; ventilation planned so agarbatti smoke doesn't stain the back panel; surfaces near the flame chosen for heat tolerance and easy cleaning; the samagri drawer sized for the actual bottles and boxes it will hold; and lighting — a warm wash over the deities — wired discreetly with the switch in reach. Where the family sits for a longer path or Satyanarayan katha, we plan the floor space and a fold-away seating ledge too.

## Materials, finish and care

Structures in quality plywood and solid wood for the crafted elements; finishes in warm polished tones (PU or melamine over veneer/solid wood) or laminates for easy-care surfaces. Backlit panels use proper LED profiles that don't heat the unit. Everything is specified in the itemised quote. Years later, when the daily diya has mellowed the finish, our [polishing](/services/polishing) team can restore it — several mandirs we built long ago have come back for exactly that, which we take as a compliment.

## A measured, unhurried process

We visit, measure, and — more than for any other furniture — listen: which deities, which rituals, morning or evening pooja, who performs it. The design and itemised quote follow. Most wall units are installed within 10-14 days; larger units and room fit-outs take two to three weeks. Installation is scheduled at a time the family chooses, and many prefer an auspicious day — entirely our pleasure to work around.
